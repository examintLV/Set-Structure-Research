# Player Analysis Guide

## Goal
Assess which set-score outcomes are more plausible for a player in a specified upcoming singles matchup. For best-of-three matches, assess the player's chances of a 2–0 win, 2–1 win, 0–2 loss, or 1–2 loss. Use best-of-five outcomes only when that is the actual match format.

This is an analytical estimate, not a guarantee. Never claim that a player will win or that a scoreline is certain. If the evidence is too thin, say so.

## 1. Confirm the matchup and format
Record both players, event, date, surface, indoor/outdoor conditions if known, and match format. Confirm the format from a reliable event source. Do not compare set counts across different formats. Note any unusual scoring rules, such as a match tiebreak replacing a final set.

## 2. Gather relevant evidence
Use verifiable match results and player statistics. Prefer recent results on the same surface, while keeping a broader sample for context. For both players, review:
- Recent match results and set scores, with dates, surface, opponent, and format.
- Serve and return indicators when reliably available: service points won, return points won, hold/break rates, and tiebreak results.
- Results against opponents of broadly comparable playing strength.
- First-set results and what happened afterward: whether the player commonly closes after winning the first set, or recovers after losing it.
- Relevant context that can be verified, such as a recent return from injury or a compressed schedule. Do not infer motivation, fitness, or injury from rumors.

Use the same time window and definitions for both players where practical. Show how many matches support each comparison. Do not treat a small run of wins, head-to-head results, or a few tiebreaks as decisive evidence. If a stat or source is unavailable, mark it unavailable rather than guessing.

## 3. Compare the matchup
Summarize the evidence that favors each player, including the opponent's strengths. Consider whether one player's serve is likely to be difficult for the other to return, whether surface-specific results support the matchup, and whether the players' set-level histories fit a straight-set or three-set match. Keep observed facts separate from interpretation.

Do not just count historical straight-set wins and call that the probability for this matchup. Historical records are context; they may differ in opponent quality, surface, conditions, and sample size. Avoid double-counting the same evidence in multiple statistics.

## 4. Assess the four outcomes
For a best-of-three match, discuss each outcome from the named player's perspective:
1. Player wins 2–0.
2. Player wins 2–1.
3. Player loses 0–2.
4. Player loses 1–2.

Identify the leading outcome or outcomes and explain why. State what could make the assessment wrong. A player can be more likely to win overall without a straight-set win being the most likely exact score; distinguish those questions.

If giving numerical probabilities, use a documented model that has been tested on separate historical matches and calibrated for the relevant format and context. Make the four outcome probabilities sum to 100%, state the model and evidence limitations, and do not manufacture a percentage from a small sample. If no suitable model is available, give a qualitative ranking (for example, more plausible / less plausible) instead of false precision.

Cite sources close to the claims they support. Do not fabricate sources, match records, statistics, or model results. Do not use sportsbook odds or betting-market prices as evidence.

## Best-of-five matches
For a best-of-five match, assess 3–0, 3–1, and 3–2 wins and 0–3, 1–3, and 2–3 losses from the player's perspective. Analyze best-of-five records separately. Do not relabel a best-of-three analysis as best-of-five.
